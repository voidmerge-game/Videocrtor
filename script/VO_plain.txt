In 1870, Africa is a continent of empires, kingdoms and city-states. The Sokoto Caliphate rules more people than any European colony on earth. The Zulu, the Asante, the Ethiopians: these are powers that have defeated European armies in the field.

Europeans control barely ten percent of the land: a string of coastal trading posts, the old Portuguese footholds in Angola and Mozambique, the Dutch and British settlements at the Cape, and France's colony in Algeria, taken in 1830.

The interior is, to European eyes, a blank space on the map. Explorers like David Livingstone have crossed it, mapped its rivers, and come home to tell a public hungry for adventure stories about a land of ivory, gold, and souls to be saved. Within one generation, that blank space will be gone.

Three things change everything. First, technology. Quinine lets Europeans survive malaria. Steamboats push up rivers that sailing ships could never climb. The telegraph and the Suez Canal, opened in 1869, shrink the distance between London, Paris and the African coast.

And in 1884, Hiram Maxim unveils a machine gun that fires six hundred rounds a minute. One British writer puts it bluntly: "Whatever happens, we have got the Maxim gun, and they have not."

Second, economics. Industrial Europe is hungry for palm oil, rubber, cotton, copper, and for new markets. Third, politics. A newly united Germany, a humiliated France, and a nervous Britain each fear that whatever they do not take, a rival will.

The spark comes from an unlikely place. Leopold the Second, King of the Belgians, rules a small country with no interest in empire. So he decides to build one for himself.

He hires the explorer Henry Morton Stanley, famous for finding Livingstone, to sail up the Congo River, build stations, and sign hundreds of treaties with local chiefs, treaties written in a language they cannot read, handing over their land to a committee in Brussels.

France and Portugal rush to stake rival claims on the same river. The great powers realize the race is on, and that without rules, it could end in a European war.

In November 1884, Chancellor Otto von Bismarck invites fourteen states to Berlin. For three months they negotiate the future of a continent. Not a single African is in the room.

The conference hands the Congo basin, an area eighty times the size of Belgium, to Leopold as his private property. And it sets the rule that will drive the next thirty years: "effective occupation."

A flag on the coast is no longer enough. To own land, a power must actually hold it. Berlin does not start the Scramble. It makes it mandatory.

Watch the map. In 1881, France seizes Tunisia. In 1882, Britain occupies Egypt to secure the Suez Canal, and with it, the road to India.

In 1884, Bismarck, who had once called colonies a luxury, suddenly claims four of them: South-West Africa, Togoland, Kamerun, and East Africa.

Britain lets chartered companies do the work: the Royal Niger Company in the north, the British South Africa Company of Cecil Rhodes in the south, who dreams of a railway from the Cape to Cairo under one flag.

France moves inland from Senegal and south from Algeria, through the oases of the Sahara, until two French columns meet in the desert and a single empire stretches from the Mediterranean to the Congo.

The two empires nearly collide. In 1898, a French expedition that has marched for two years from the Atlantic plants its flag at Fashoda, on the Upper Nile, and finds Kitchener's gunboats waiting. For weeks, Britain and France stand on the edge of war. Paris backs down. Within six years, the two rivals are allies, united by a greater fear: Germany.

Italy takes Eritrea and a strip of Somalia. Portugal pushes inland to join its old coastal colonies. Spain picks up the Western Sahara.

By 1900, ninety percent of Africa is under European flags. In thirty years, a continent has changed hands.

None of this happens without a fight. In 1879, a Zulu army annihilates a British column at Isandlwana, the worst defeat a modern European army has suffered at African hands.

In West Africa, Samori Touré fights the French for sixteen years, manufacturing his own rifles, moving his entire state eastward to stay out of reach.

In Sudan, the Mahdi's army takes Khartoum in 1885 and holds the country for thirteen years, until Kitchener's Maxim guns at Omdurman kill ten thousand men in a single morning, for the loss of forty-eight British soldiers.

And then there is Ethiopia. In 1896, at Adwa, Emperor Menelik the Second, armed with modern rifles bought from the very Europeans who were dividing his neighbors, destroys an Italian army of seventeen thousand men. Ethiopia stays independent. On the whole continent, only Liberia can say the same.

Conquest is only the beginning. In Leopold's Congo, villages are given rubber quotas. Those who fail are flogged, taken hostage, or killed. Soldiers are ordered to account for every bullet with a severed hand.

Missionaries and a shipping clerk named Edmund Morel expose the system to the world. By the time Belgium takes the colony from its king in 1908, the population of the Congo has fallen by millions.

In German South-West Africa, the Herero and Nama rise in 1904. The German commander issues an extermination order. Survivors are driven into the desert or worked to death in camps. Up to eighty percent of the Herero people die.

Even Europeans turn on Europeans. In South Africa, Britain's war against the Boer republics costs more than twenty thousand lives in British concentration camps, most of them children.

The last pieces fall in the decade before the Great War. Twice, in 1905 and 1911, Germany challenges France over Morocco, sending a gunboat to Agadir and bringing Europe to the brink. Twice, Germany is forced to step back, and each crisis pushes the continent closer to 1914.

By 1914, the map is finished. France has taken Morocco, Italy has taken Libya from the Ottomans, and seven European powers rule everything except Ethiopia and Liberia.

Then look again. The borders drawn in Berlin, London and Paris, often with a ruler, cutting through more than a hundred and seventy peoples, are the borders Africa still lives with today.

When the colonies won their independence in the 1950s and 60s, their new leaders faced a choice: redraw the map along the lines of the peoples who lived there, or keep the frontiers they had inherited. In 1964, they chose to keep them, judging that any attempt to redraw them would mean endless war.

The Scramble lasted thirty years. Its map has lasted a hundred and forty.
